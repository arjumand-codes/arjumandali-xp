What actually changes in a website redesign

A before/after breakdown of a client site — spacing, type scale, and one bad color decision that was quietly hurting conversions. What I changed, and why each change mattered more than a full rebuild would have.

The client's site wasn't broken — it worked, it loaded fine, and the content was solid. But it looked like it was built in 2016 and never revisited. Inconsistent spacing, three different accent colors competing for attention, and body text set at a size that made every page feel denser than it needed to. Here's what actually changed, and why a redesign doesn't have to mean starting over.

The brief

The client didn't want a rebuild — new CMS, new structure, and rewritten content were all off the table. They wanted the same information to look credible instead of dated, and they wanted the contact form to actually get used more. That's a much more common request than a full redesign, and it's usually the right instinct.

Spacing did most of the work

The single biggest visual change was also the least glamorous: consistent spacing. The original site had sections crammed together with 20px of padding in some places and 80px in others, with no clear rhythm. I set up a consistent spacing scale — small, medium, large, section-level — and applied it everywhere.

Consistent spacing alone can make a site look 40% more "designed" without touching a single color or font.

That one change gave every section room to breathe and made the page feel intentional instead of assembled from templates.

Fixing the type scale

The original site used body text and headings that were too close in size — a 22px heading over 18px body text doesn't give the eye enough contrast to scan the page quickly. I set a proper type scale: a display font for headings with real weight and size contrast, and a slightly smaller, more legible body font with generous line-height. Readers could now scan a page in seconds instead of having to read every line to find what mattered.

The color decision that was hurting conversions

Here's the one that actually mattered for the business: the site used the same bright red for "urgent" sale banners, standard call-to-action buttons, and error messages. Visually, every button looked like a warning. Analytics showed people hesitating on the main contact button — hovering, but not clicking as often as the traffic numbers suggested they should.

  • Sale/urgency banners kept their high-contrast color, used sparingly.
  • The primary call-to-action button switched to a single, consistent accent color used only for the main action on any given page.
  • Error and warning states got their own distinct, less aggressive color so they didn't compete visually with buttons people were supposed to click confidently.

Giving the main call-to-action its own dedicated color — used nowhere else on the page — made it immediately clear what the "next step" was on every screen.

What stayed the same

Just as important as what changed: the URLs, the page structure, the written content, and the underlying WordPress setup stayed exactly the same. That kept existing SEO rankings intact and meant the client didn't have to re-approve or rewrite a single paragraph — the redesign was purely a visual and structural polish pass, not a rebuild.

Frequently asked questions

Common signs are inconsistent spacing and fonts, a color palette that fights itself, a mobile version that feels like an afterthought, and visitors bouncing quickly despite decent traffic. If it looks dated compared to competitors, that's usually the tipping point.

A focused visual redesign — new spacing, typography, colors, and component styling without a full rebuild — usually takes 1-2 weeks depending on how many pages and sections are involved.

Not if it's done carefully. Keeping the same URLs, headings, and core content structure while improving the visual layer preserves your existing SEO equity, and better load times and mobile usability can actually improve rankings over time.
